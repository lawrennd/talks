\notes{\section{Opening: AI as a Diligence Problem}}

\notes{Welcome. Forty minutes on how to read AI inside portfolio companies —
not as a feature checklist, but as a change in how firms produce,
decide, and own consequence. The through-line is the *judgment layer*:
the organisational interface where human authority still decides when
the answer matters. I will keep this practical for diligence and value
creation. The useful conversation is the Q&A that follows.}

\newslide{Today's Arc}

\slidesincremental{
* What AI is — and what it is not
* How AI changes the firm
* The judgment layer — and what diligence should underwrite
}

\notes{Three beats. First, a short model of machine versus human
intelligence — without it, portfolio AI stories drift into hype.
Second, what that means inside firms: information flows, attention,
intellectual debt, and trust. Third, how agentic systems change the
diligence question: advantage and risk become organisational. The close
is a set of underwriting questions, not a product pitch.}

\notes{For investors, the Atomic Human is a diligence filter. If a
capability is easily automated — code generation, boilerplate support,
routine analytics — it will not remain a durable differentiator.
Advantage concentrates in what remains irreducibly human: judgment under
uncertainty, customer trust, category creation, and the organisational
culture that turns technology into a business.}

\newslide{Implication for Diligence}

\slidesincremental{
* AI commoditises *production* of many artefacts
* It does not create the *judgment layer*
* Durable value sits where humans still own consequence
}

\notes{Generating software, copy, forecasts, and workflows is becoming
cheaper and faster. That is not the same as knowing which product to
build, for whom, and with what accountability when it fails. The
judgment layer is that accountability. Diligence should separate
companies that use AI to ship more of the same from those that redesign
the firm around a legible judgment layer.}

\section{Part 2: How AI Changes the Firm}

\notes{AI changes the topography of information inside the firm: more
throughput, more interfaces, more automation of the middle of the stack.
The investment question is whether the organisation still owns the
decisions that matter when those interfaces fail.}

ops load, in customer trust, and in the opacity of failure modes.}

\notes{\subsection{Intellectual Debt}}

\include{_ai/includes/intellectual-debt-short.md}

\notes{Technical debt is familiar. Intellectual debt is the cousin AI
accelerates: systems that work, ship, and bill — but that nobody can
fully explain. In diligence that shows up as model-driven pricing,
support bots, recommendation engines, and agentic workflows whose
failure modes are opaque to management and to the board.}

\notes{
\subsection{Where Superficial Automation Fails}

\include{_business/includes/superficial-automation.md}
\include{_ai/includes/atrophy-and-cognitive-flattening.md}

\notes{Much of the current AI wave automates the visible surface — ticket
summaries, CRM notes, code autocomplete — while leaving the underlying
decision problem untouched. Done well, that frees attention. Done badly,
it flattens cognition inside the firm: juniors stop learning, seniors
stop reviewing, and the company loses the expertise that made the
product valuable.}
}

\notes{\subsection{A Warning on Trust}}

\include{_software/includes/horizon-scandal.md}

\notes{Horizon is a software-accountability story. When organisations
subordinate human judgment to system outputs, trust collapses. For any
portfolio company selling into regulated or mission-critical
environments, intelligent accountability is part of the product — not a
compliance afterthought. Horizon is what happens when the judgment layer
is present on paper but absent in practice.}

\section{Part 3: Judgment, Attention, and Advantage}

\notes{Looking forward, the important shift is not that machines become
digital humans. It is that human-analogue machines change the interface
between people and work — and therefore what investors should underwrite.}

\newslide{From Features to Interfaces}

\slidesincremental{
* Generative AI as a human-analogue machine (HAM)
* Information amplifier — not a substitute for judgment
* Old org charts and diligence checklists become obsolete
}

\include{_ai/includes/processor-ham.md}
\include{_data-science/includes/new-flow-of-information-ham.md}

\include{_business/includes/the-productivity-flywheel.md}
\include{_business/includes/attention-flywheel.md}

\notes{Classical productivity flywheels reinvest capital into sales and
R&D. In the AI era the scarce input is human attention — of engineers,
operators, and customers. Firms that free attention and then waste it on
more shallow automation will not compound. Firms that reinvest attention
into judgment, relationships, and new product categories will. For
investors, that is the value-creation test: where does freed attention
go?}

\include{_business/includes/ft-op-ed.md}

\notes{The FT piece — *AI cannot replace the atomic human* — is the
shortest public statement of this point. Attempting to measure human
capital poses a productivity paradox: what machines can quantify, they
can automate. The remainder that matters for portfolio value is judgment,
trust, and care for the customer problem.}

\define{agenticDebtOneSlide}
\include{_ai/includes/agentic-debt-short.md}

\notes{Agentic systems — systems that can act inside workflows — may pay
down technical and intellectual debt. They can also create agentic debt:
delegation without clear authority, evidence, or recovery. For investors,
the governance question becomes: who or what can cause what action, on
what basis, with what stop-condition?}

\notes{\subsection{The Judgment Layer}

This is the pivot. Once agents execute real workflows, advantage and
risk stop being model properties and become organisational ones. The
right diligence question is not how to make the model produce the right
answer. It is who has the authority to decide when the answer matters.}

\include{_ai/includes/judgment-layer-explicit.md}

\newslide{Don't Mark Your Own Homework}

\slidesincremental{
* Cybersecurity is where consequence is sharpest
* You do not want the builder marking their own homework
* That is why we founded Trent — judgment separated from execution
}

\notes{Cybersecurity makes the judgment-layer argument concrete. When the
same organisation writes the system, runs the system, and adjudicates
whether the system is safe, authorship and accountability collapse into
each other. That is marking your own homework. In security, that is how
failures become invisible until they are catastrophic. The judgment layer
has to sit apart from automated execution — with clear authority,
evidence, and a stop-condition. That separation is why we founded Trent.}

agentic AI — proprietary and commercial — while keeping authority with
the people who own consequence.}

\section{Close: What Diligence Should Underwrite}

\newslide{Strategic Priorities for Investors}

\slidesincremental{
* People first, not model first
* Ask whether the judgment layer is *explicit*
* Underwrite authority where consequence lives
* Watch for intellectual *and* agentic debt
}

\notes{A short forward look for the room. As code generation and workflow
automation spread, the marginal cost of production falls. That elevates
everything around the artefact: proprietary data, distribution, customer
trust, and the judgment to know what should be built — and when to halt.
Underwrite the judgment layer as carefully as the model layer.}

\include{_business/includes/ft-letter-workflow-by-workflow.md}

\notes{The FT letter is aimed at portfolio thinking. Markets err when they
value a group of businesses as if AI were one centralised programme.
Value arrives workflow by workflow, in the hands of people who understand
the work. That is another way of saying: underwrite the *local* judgment
layer — do not assume the centre can create the operational change once
for all.}

\newslide{Takeaways}

\slidesincremental{
* AI commoditises many artefacts — not the judgment layer
* Advantage sits in attention, trust, and *legible* human authority
* Intellectual and agentic debt are balance-sheet risks
* Diligence should underwrite judgment as carefully as the model
}
