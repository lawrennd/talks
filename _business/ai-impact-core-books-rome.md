\section{Opening: from Internet Era to AI Era}

\newslide{From the Internet era to the AI era}

\slides{
* The Internet scaled *communication*.
* Platforms scaled *mediation* of information.
* AI scales *prediction and decision*.
}

\speakernotes{Paolo Cellini’s *AI Impact* starts from the Internet era: ICT, networks, platforms and then AI as the next platform shift. My contribution is to connect that history to a human question: what changes when prediction and decision become scalable infrastructure?}

\section{The triad and the “prediction engine”}

\newslide{Cellini’s triad → prediction at scale}

\slides{
* **Data** + **model** + **compute** → **prediction**
* Prediction at scale → **decision** at scale
* That makes AI a new layer of *information infrastructure*
}

\speakernotes{Cellini’s triad (data/algorithms/compute) is a useful way to stay grounded: nothing happens without those three. In the Atomic Human framing, that triad becomes a prediction engine. When it’s deployed inside platforms and organisations, prediction becomes decision—recommendations, rankings, allocations, credit, insurance, hiring, and governance.}

\newslide{From Marconi to AI}

\slides{
* Marconi made *communication* wireless and scalable
* AI makes *prediction and decision* scalable
* The question shifts from "can we transmit?" to "what do we transmit *and trust*?"
}

\speakernotes{This is the key bridge: an infrastructure shift. When decision is separated from place—and sometimes from institutional responsibility—we need governance, not mysticism.}

\section{What goes wrong: feedback loops, attention, concentration}

\newslide{Feedback loops in the information ecosystem}

\include{_atomic-human/includes/information-flows-diagram.md}

\speakernotes{This figure captures a simple risk: models ingest human-created data and reflect it back, creating feedback loops that can distort discourse.}

\speakernotes{Cellini’s market-structure chapter aligns with a central risk: concentration. When compute and data concentrate, the ability to set defaults, shape demand, and mediate what is visible concentrates too. That’s not just competition policy; it’s epistemic security.}

\section{Cooperation: trust, autonomy, accountability}

\newslide{Cooperation design principles}

\slides{
* **Keep humans on the hook** (named accountability)
* **Make delegation conditional** (audit, escalation, reversibility)
* **Protect meaning** (provenance, consent, compensation)
}

\speakernotes{The cooperation question is practical. It’s not “trust AI” or “ban AI”. It’s: where does delegation help, where is human judgment essential, and how do we make responsibility legible in complex systems?}

\speakernotes{We should also remember infrastructure constraints (compute, chips, energy, location) shape what is feasible and governable, which is why Cellini’s triad is such a good grounding.}
