\subsection{Human Capital and Economic Measurement}

\notes{Central banks rely on measuring economic variables to guide policy. But as AI reshapes work, the very concept of "human capital" becomes problematic. The Human Capital Index attempts to quantify the economic value of a population's skills and health. But what happens when we try to optimize this metric directly? We encounter a version of Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.}

\subsection{AI Agents and Economic Agency}

\notes{As we move from passive language models to agentic AI systems that can act in markets, make decisions, and allocate resources, we face new questions for economic policy. These agents don't have human constraints, they don't need sleep, don't experience fatigue, can process vast information flows. But they also don't have human judgment, contextual understanding, or ethical frameworks developed over centuries of economic practice. They also sit clumsily within institutional structures we have built to scale these human characteristics.}

\subsection{The Attention Economy and Financial Markets}

\notes{The attention economy operates as a new form of capital accumulation. Tech platforms accumulate attention, convert it to data, use that data to improve AI systems, which capture more attention. For financial regulators, this raises questions about market concentration, systemic risk, and the role of data as collateral. When attention becomes the scarce resource, how do we think about monetary policy in an economy where the primary currency is human attention, not central bank money?}

\subsection{Economic Models and Human Complexity}

\notes{Professor Cellini will discuss economic models of artificial intelligence. The challenge these models face is that AI systems operate in fundamentally different ways than the economic agents in our models. Traditional economic models assume rational agents with preferences, constraints, and the ability to optimize. AI systems have different types of rationality—statistical patterns learned from data rather than logical deduction from principles. This creates a modeling challenge: how do we build economic models that capture the interaction between human economic agents and AI systems?}

\slides{* Traditional Models: Rational Agents
* AI Systems: Statistical Patterns
* Challenge: Modeling Hybrid Intelligence
* Question: What's the New Equilibrium?}

\subsection{Implications for Economic Policy}

\notes{For central banks, AI presents both opportunities and challenges. On one hand, AI can improve forecasting, risk assessment, and market surveillance. On the other hand, AI may fundamentally change the transmission mechanisms of monetary policy. If wage-setting increasingly involves AI systems, if price discovery happens through algorithmic markets, if credit allocation is mediated by machine learning models, then the traditional tools of monetary policy may work differently. The atomic human—the individual human with unique judgment, context, and values—remains essential precisely because these qualities cannot be automated.}

\slides{* Opportunities: Forecasting, Risk, Surveillance
* Challenges: New Transmission Mechanisms
* Wage-Setting by Algorithm?
* Credit Allocation by ML?
* Human Judgment Still Essential}

\subsection{The Path Forward}

\notes{The key insight is that AI cannot replace the atomic human—not because of technical limitations, but because of fundamental economic and social reasons. Human judgment, contextual understanding, and ethical reasoning remain essential for economic decision-making, particularly in uncertain environments. Central banks and financial institutions must find ways to harness AI's capabilities while preserving human agency and judgment. This requires new frameworks for thinking about human-AI collaboration, new approaches to measuring economic performance, and new institutional structures that ensure AI serves societal needs rather than just optimizing narrow metrics.}

\slides{* AI Cannot Replace Atomic Human
* Not Technical Limitation—Fundamental Necessity
* Human Judgment in Uncertainty
* Central Banks' Role: Framework Design
* Goal: AI Serving Society, Not Metrics}
