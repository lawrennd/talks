\section{Part 1: What AI Is and Isn't}

\include{_ai/includes/henry-ford-intro.md}

\notes{As we enter an era where machines increasingly mimic tasks traditionally undertaken by humans, students of corporate strategy need to understand what is actually changing — and what is not. The challenges aren't merely operational; they require us to reimagine the nature of competitive advantage, resources and capabilities, and the scope of the firm.}

\subsection{Artificial General Vehicle}

\include{_atomic-human/includes/artificial-general-vehicle-diagram.md}

\notes{From a strategic perspective, one of the most dangerous misconceptions about AI is the idea of artificial general intelligence — a machine that can do everything a human can do. This framing leads firms to make poor investments and misjudge the competitive landscape. The Artificial General Vehicle analogy helps correct this.}

\subsection{Embodiment Factors: The Information Bandwidth Gap}

\include{_ai/includes/embodiment-factors-celsius.md}

\notes{These bandwidth differences are strategically significant. Humans process rich sensory, emotional and social information at low bandwidth — but that information is extraordinarily dense with context. Machines process vast amounts of data at high bandwidth but without the contextual grounding that gives meaning. This is not a temporary gap that will close; it reflects a fundamental difference in how human and machine intelligence are embodied.}

\include{_ai/includes/baby-shoes.md}

\notes{The baby shoes example captures what machines miss: the ability to understand meaning through context, emotion and shared human experience. For corporate strategy, this matters because many of the most valuable activities in an organisation — building client relationships, exercising ethical judgment, interpreting ambiguous situations — depend precisely on these capacities.}

\subsection{The Atomic Human}

\include{_economics/includes/homo-atomicus.md}

\notes{The Atomic Human is the irreducible core of human intelligence and agency that AI cannot replicate. Moving away from *homo economicus* — the rational utility-maximiser — toward *homo atomicus* changes how we think about human capital, talent strategy, and what it means to build an organisation.}

\subsection{Discussion}

\slides{
* What is the indivisible human contribution to your organisation?
* Where does AI capability end and human judgment begin?
}

\section{Part 2: Information, Institutions and Change}

\subsection{AI as the Modern Philosopher's Stone}

\include{_economics/includes/philosophers-stone.md}

\notes{During the industrial revolution, steel and steam replaced human manual labour. Today, silicon and electrons are being combined to replace human mental labour. This is the strategic megatrend underlying AI — the automation not of physical work but of cognitive work. Understanding this positions it correctly within the strategy frameworks you have studied.}

\subsection{The Attention Economy}

\include{_economics/includes/the-attention-economy.md}

\include{_economics/includes/herbert-simon-information.md}

\notes{Herbert Simon's insight — that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention — describes the fundamental constraint that shapes how organisations are designed and how strategy is executed. In a world of AI-generated information abundance, human attention becomes the scarce resource around which corporate strategy must be built.}

\subsection{How AI Changes Information Flows}

\include{_data-science/includes/new-flow-of-information.md}

\notes{AI does not just automate tasks — it restructures the information flows within and between organisations. Understanding your firm's information topography becomes a strategic capability. Where information pools, where it flows freely, and where it is blocked determines where AI creates value and where it creates risk.}

\subsection{Institutional Character and Conway's Law}

\include{_business/includes/institutional-character.md}

\notes{Conway's Law tells us that organisations produce systems that mirror their communication structures. This has a direct implication for AI strategy: you cannot deploy AI effectively without understanding and potentially redesigning your organisational structure. The technology does not arrive into a vacuum — it interacts with existing institutional arrangements, power structures and cultures.}

\subsection{Imposed vs Organic Institutions}

\include{_atomic-human/includes/legacy-institutions-vs-organic-growth.md}

\notes{When we think about institutional change in response to AI, the distinction between imposed and organically grown institutions matters enormously. Organisations that try to adopt AI by imposing structures designed elsewhere — copying Silicon Valley models wholesale — often waste energy adapting to foreign models rather than building on local strengths. Sustainable AI adoption builds on what the institution already does well.}

\subsection{Trust, Autonomy and Embodiment}

\include{_atomic-human/includes/trust-autonomy-embodiment.md}

\notes{Trust is the connective tissue of institutions. When we deploy AI systems, we are making decisions about where to place trust — in the algorithm, in the human, or in some combination. Getting this balance wrong has consequences that range from lost productivity to serious ethical failures.}

\subsection{A Warning: The Horizon Scandal}

\include{_software/includes/horizon-scandal.md}

\notes{The Horizon scandal is one of the most important case studies in the failure of AI governance. Hundreds of Post Office sub-postmasters were wrongfully prosecuted because institutional trust was placed in a flawed software system over the testimony of individuals. For corporate strategy students, this is a lesson in Week 7's themes: institutional culture, ethics, and the governance of automated systems. The question is not only whether AI works — it is whether the institutional structures exist to catch it when it does not.}

\subsection{Discussion}

\slides{
* Where does your institution's structure help or hinder its response to AI?
* What mechanisms exist to keep human judgment in the loop?
}

\section{Part 3: Strategic Implications}

\subsection{The Productivity Flywheel}

\include{_business/includes/the-productivity-flywheel.md}

\notes{The traditional productivity flywheel assumes that gains from automation are reinvested as financial capital, which funds further innovation. But AI's effect on human capital — the devaluation of cognitive skills — disrupts this model. A new flywheel is needed.}

\subsection{The Attention Reinvestment Cycle}

\include{_business/includes/attention-flywheel.md}

\notes{The attention flywheel reframes productivity gains from AI not as financial returns to be reinvested in more automation, but as human attention to be reinvested in higher-value human activities. For corporate strategy, this changes how you think about digital transformation: not as cost reduction alone, but as liberation of human capacity for activities that machines cannot do.}

\subsection{The Supply Chain of Ideas}

\include{_business/includes/supply-chain-of-ideas.md}

\notes{Just as physical supply chains connect production to consumption, there is a supply chain of ideas that connects innovation to application. A key failure in many AI strategies is over-investing in the supply side — creating new technology — without building the demand-side infrastructure to deploy it effectively. Corporate strategy needs to map both sides of this chain.}

\subsection{Where Should AI Be Directed?}

\include{_atomic-human/includes/societys-wicked-problems-diagram.md}

\notes{The closing provocation: AI's most important strategic role may not be in optimising what firms already do, but in addressing the problems that have been too hard, too expensive, or too complex to solve without it. Society's wicked problems — in health, education, social care, climate — represent both a moral challenge and a strategic opportunity for organisations prepared to engage with them.}
