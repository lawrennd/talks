\section{Opening: Commercial Advantage in the Age of Intelligent Machines}

\notes{Welcome. Over the next ninety minutes we will look at artificial
intelligence from a commercial perspective: what recent systems can and cannot
do, how they change the firm, and what leaders should do next. Simultaneous
interpretation is running, so I will keep the beats short and leave time for
discussion.}

intelligence — without that, commercial strategy drifts into hype. Second,
how AI reshapes information flows, attention, and decision rights inside the
firm. Third, recent developments — generative systems as human-analogue
machines — and the strategic choices that follow.}

\notes{For commercial leaders, the Atomic Human is not a philosophical
aside. It is a strategy filter. If a capability is easily automated, it will
not remain a durable source of differentiation. Advantage concentrates in the
parts of the business that remain irreducibly human: trust, judgment under
uncertainty, culture, and the relationships that bind customers and partners.}

\section{Part 3: Recent Developments and Future Directions}

\notes{The recent wave of generative systems is commercially important
because it changes the *interface* between humans and machines, not
because it creates a digital human. Treat large language models as
human-analogue machines: powerful amplifiers of information that still
require human authorship for consequence. But the market story is not
“bigger models only”: what is compounding is harnesses and small models
on cognitive toil, under a Good Regulator constraint.}

\subsection{Future Directions: Opportunity and New Forms of Debt}

\notes{Before agents that act, one constraint from cybernetics. If the firm
is still meant to regulate what its systems do, the people who own the risk
must still be able to model those systems. That is the Good Regulator
condition, and it is why we minimise.}

\notes{Agentic systems — systems that can act — may pay down technical
and intellectual debt. They can also create agentic debt: delegation
without clear authority, evidence, or recovery. For CEOs, the governance
question is becoming: who or what can cause what action, on what basis,
with what stop-condition?}
